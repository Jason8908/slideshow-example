Systematic review & meta-analysis

Intra-arterial lidocaine as a pain management strategy for uterine artery embolisation

Tarun Haridasan1, Abena Kena1, Larry Nguyen1 and Kiat Tsong Tan1,2

1Michael G. DeGroote School of Medicine, McMaster University, Hamilton, ON, Canada
2Department of Medical Imaging, Niagara Health System, St. Catharines, ON, Canada

CVIR Endovascular 2026;9:121 · doi:10.1186/s42155-026-00771-y · Open Access, CC BY 4.0

Today I'm presenting a systematic review and meta-analysis published this year in CVIR Endovascular by Haridasan, Kena, Nguyen and Tan from McMaster and Niagara Health.

The question: does injecting lidocaine directly into the uterine artery during embolisation reduce the pain that follows, and is it safe?

The image is a schematic angiogram: contrast filling a tortuous uterine artery feeding a fibroid "blush".

Background

~30%

  • Heavy uterine bleeding
  • Anaemia
  • Back pain
  • Constipation

More common, larger, more numerous and earlier-onset in women of African ancestry

Fibroids (leiomyomas) are benign monoclonal growths of the myometrium, affecting 70–80% of women over 50.

Most are asymptomatic and found incidentally, but around 30% develop severe symptoms (bleeding, anaemia, back pain, constipation, infertility) that need intervention.

Risk factors include obesity, hypertension, early menarche, nulliparity, older age and family history. Fibroids are more common, larger and earlier in women of African ancestry.

Background

UAE preserves the uterus, but pain is the main hurdle

  1. 1A microcatheter is guided into the uterine artery
  2. 2Embolic particles block the fibroid's blood supply
  3. 3The fibroid infarcts and shrinks

Ischaemic pain is most intense in the first hours after the procedure

Surgery, including hysterectomy, has been the conventional treatment. Uterine artery embolisation is a minimally invasive alternative for patients who want to keep the uterus and fertility.

Click through: catheter in the uterine artery → embolic particles occlude the feeders → the fibroid infarcts.

The trade-off is pain: ischaemia produces intense pain within the first few hours. That's the problem this review addresses.

Background

Intra-arterial lidocaine is promising, but contested

Systemic

Opioid regimens

Systemic

NSAIDs

Regional

Superior hypogastric nerve block

Intra-arterial

Lidocaine (IAL)

Given through the same microcatheter, during or right after embolisation

Common strategies for post-UAE pain

Why it is still debated

  • Inconsistent benefit for pain and opioid use
  • Neurological symptoms, arrhythmias and, rarely, seizures reported
  • Possible uterine artery vasospasm, affecting embolic delivery

Options include opioid regimens, NSAIDs and superior hypogastric nerve blocks.

Intra-arterial lidocaine is attractive because it goes through the microcatheter that's already in place, during or immediately after embolisation.

But the evidence is mixed: some studies showed minimal effect on pain and opioid use, some reported neurological symptoms, arrhythmias and rarely seizures, and there's concern lidocaine might cause vasospasm and compromise embolic delivery.

Objective

Does intra-arterial lidocaine reduce pain after UAE, and is it safe?

Population
Adult women undergoing uterine artery embolisation
Intervention
Intra-arterial lidocaine as the primary pain strategy
Comparison
Control: no intra-arterial lidocaine
Outcomes
Primary: post-procedure pain (VAS, NRS)
Secondary: opioid use, complications, readmissions, length of stay

Because results have been inconsistent, the authors ran an updated systematic review and meta-analysis.

PICO: adult women undergoing UAE; IAL as the primary pain strategy; compared with no IAL.

Primary outcome: post-procedure pain on VAS or NRS. Secondary: opioid consumption, length of stay, time to discharge, satisfaction, technical success, complications and readmissions.

Eligible designs: RCTs, prospective or retrospective cohorts, case–control studies · English-language full text

Conducted per PRISMA 2020 and registered on PROSPERO (CRD420261428710).

Search: MEDLINE, Embase and Cochrane from inception to 19 June 2026, using MeSH terms and keywords (uterine artery embolisation, lidocaine, lignocaine…), plus reference lists.

Screening and extraction in Covidence by two independent reviewers, with a third resolving disagreements.

Risk of bias: RoB 2 for RCTs, ROBINS-I for non-randomised studies.

RevMan 5.4, random effects throughout; MD with inverse variance for continuous outcomes, I² for heterogeneity. Medians/IQRs were converted to means/SDs. For Cai, only the bilateral IAL arm was used; Noel-Lamy's two IAL arms were combined per the Cochrane Handbook.

Eight studies from 2001 to 2025, 692 patients: four RCTs and four retrospective cohorts.

Five countries: China (3), USA (2), Canada, Japan and Korea.

Seven studied fibroids; Cai 2025 (the largest, n = 255) studied UAE for caesarean scar pregnancy, a clinically different population. Keep it in mind for the sensitivity analysis.

Pain was measured with VAS in six studies, NRS in one and a six-level ordinal scale in one.

Intervention characteristics

No two lidocaine protocols were alike

Data from Table 3 · Hover a dot to trace one study across rows

A major source of heterogeneity: concentration ranged from 0.5% to 2%, dose from 40 to 200 mg and volume from 4 to 20 mL.

Timing varied too: before embolisation (Keyoung, Cai), mixed with microspheres during UAE (Tang, one Noel-Lamy arm), or immediately after (the rest).

Embolic agents also differed: polyvinyl alcohol, tris-acryl gelatin microspheres and gelatin sponge. There were too few studies for subgroup analyses by these variables.

Pain scores could be pooled at 0, 4, 7 and 24 hours. Points below zero favour lidocaine.

0 h: MD −0.54 (−2.03 to 0.96), 3 studies, I² 96%: not significant.

4 h: MD −1.07 (−2.13 to −0.01), P = 0.05: significant, though borderline and with I² 82%.

7 h: −0.36, I² 0%. 24 h: −0.09, I² 24%. Neither significant.

Peak pain within 24 h (highest score at any time point, 6 studies, 511 patients): MD −1.37 (−2.30 to −0.45), P = 0.004.

Secondary outcomes

Other opioids and inflammatory markers: no clear signal

Other opioids single studies, not pooled

Inflammatory markers at 24 h 2 studies · 127 patients

Data from Results and Supplementary Figs S5–S6 ; units as reported

Two studies used other opioids, so they couldn't be pooled with morphine. Alqahtani found higher 24-h fentanyl use with IAL (1037 vs 881 µg); Tang found lower 24-h sufentanil use (38.3 vs 45.7).

Two studies (127 patients) reported 24-h CRP and WBC: no significant difference in either (CRP MD −5.58, P = 0.23; WBC MD −1.14, P = 0.13).

Sensitivity analysis

Excluding the caesarean scar pregnancy cohort strengthened the effect

Cai 2025 treated a clinically distinct population: UAE for caesarean scar pregnancy rather than fibroids

Data from Results and Supplementary Figs S7–S8

With Cai removed, heterogeneity dropped and the effect got larger.

4-h pain: MD −1.07 → −1.52 (−2.58 to −0.46), P = 0.005; I² 82% → 56%.

Peak 24-h pain: MD −1.37 → −1.71 (−2.84 to −0.57), P = 0.003; I² 81% → 75%.

So the early benefit is not driven by the outlier population; if anything it was diluted by it.

Reporting was inconsistent and samples were small, so firm safety conclusions are not possible

Most studies reported no major complications attributable to IAL.

The headline concern comes from Keyoung 2001: severe vasospasm in 70% and early termination. But that was one of the earliest studies and later work didn't reproduce it; Zhan and Tang saw only mild vasospasm.

Alqahtani described one transient bradycardia with loss of consciousness, resolved with atropine. Duvnjak had patients phone in with severe pain on day three, with no readmissions.

Bottom line: reassuring, but reporting was inconsistent and samples small.

Study quality was mixed

Data from Figs 5–6 , assessed with RoB 2 and ROBINS-I · Hover a column for its domain

RCTs (RoB 2): Noel-Lamy was low risk in every domain; Duvnjak, Zhan and Keyoung had some concerns, mainly selection of the reported result.

Non-randomised (ROBINS-I): Tang and Katsumori moderate; Cai and Alqahtani serious, driven by confounding (D1).

Discussion

Why does the benefit fade?

  1. 1

    Embolisation causes ischaemic pain that peaks in the first hours

  2. 2

    Lidocaine is thought to suppress pain signals from the preserved myometrial branches

  3. 3

    Its short half-life may explain why the effect is gone by 7–24 h

Opioid use was unchanged: short duration of action, plus opioid protocols that differed between centres

Consistent with prior work: a meta-analysis of RCTs also found less pain at 4 h only

Post-procedure pain comes from ischaemia. Lidocaine is thought to suppress the pain response in the remaining, preserved myometrial branches.

The greatest pain occurs in the first several hours, which is exactly where IAL helped. The lack of effect later is consistent with lidocaine's short half-life.

Why no opioid sparing? Short duration of action, plus variable opioid protocols. Only morphine studies could be pooled.

This matches Liu & Li's earlier meta-analysis of RCTs: reduced pain at 4 h, no effect later.

Discussion

What would settle the question

Standardise

Large RCTs with one protocol

Concentration (0.5–2%), dose (40–200 mg), volume (4–20 mL) and timing all varied, and there were too few studies for subgroup analyses.

Extend

Longer-acting agents

Intra-arterial ropivacaine kept pain mild through the procedure and early recovery, though so far in a single case report .

Heterogeneity is partly protocol-driven: lidocaine concentration, dose, volume, timing and embolic agent all varied. With so few studies, subgroup analyses weren't feasible. We need RCTs with standardised protocols and pain scales.

Lidocaine's short action suggests trying a longer-acting agent. Ho & Tan recently described intra-arterial ropivacaine: the patient had only mild pain through the procedure and early post-procedure period. That's one case, but it targets the main limitation found here.

Limitations

Read with caution

  • I² up to 96%

    Heterogeneity in design, population and lidocaine protocol

  • 8 · 692

    Small evidence base: 8 studies and 692 patients limit statistical power

  • 4 of 8

    Retrospective studies, 2 of them at serious risk of bias

  • 1 of 8

    Distinct population: caesarean scar pregnancy, partly addressed by sensitivity analysis

Moderate to substantial heterogeneity in design, population and IAL protocol. Removing Cai helped but didn't eliminate it.

Only 8 studies and 692 patients, so limited power.

Half the studies were retrospective, and two of those were at serious risk of bias.

So: interpret with caution, and larger standardised RCTs are needed.

Conclusion

Take-home messages

  • Meaningful early relief

    Pain 1.07 points lower at 4 h; peak 24-h pain 1.37 points lower

  • No effect beyond the early window

    No difference at 0, 7 or 24 h, in opioid use, or in inflammatory markers

  • Favourable, but unproven, safety

    Serious events were rare; more studies are needed to confirm safety

IAL is a technically simple adjunct to, not a replacement for, systemic analgesia

One: IAL gives meaningful early pain relief, at 4 h and for peak pain in the first 24 h.

Two: no benefit for later pain, opioid consumption or inflammatory markers.

Three: the safety profile looks favourable, but more studies are needed.

Given its technical simplicity, IAL can act as an adjunct to existing protocols, not a replacement for systemic analgesia. Larger, standardised trials are needed to define its role.
